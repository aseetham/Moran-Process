# Moran Process Simulation

Companion code for Chapter 6 of Martin Nowak's *Evolutionary Dynamics*.
This project simulates how a single mutant spreads (or doesn't) through 
a finite population, and verifies the analytical formulas against 
empirical simulation.

## What I learned

### The Moran process

A population of N individuals contains two types: A (mutant) with fitness r 
and B (resident) with fitness 1. At each time step, one individual is chosen 
to reproduce (weighted by fitness) and one is chosen to die (uniformly). 
The offspring replaces the dead one, so N stays constant.

Eventually the population reaches one of two absorbing states: either the 
mutant goes extinct (state 0) or the mutant takes over (state N). The 
probability of taking over is the **fixation probability**:

    rho = (1 - 1/r) / (1 - 1/r^N)        for r != 1
    rho = 1/N                              for r = 1 (neutral)

A surprising consequence: even a beneficial mutation (r > 1) usually 
goes extinct by chance when there's only one copy of it. With N = 100 
and r = 1.1, only about 9% of beneficial mutants actually fix.

### Two views of evolution: neutral vs adaptive

The **rate of substitution** — how often a new mutation actually fixes 
and replaces the resident — is:

    R = N * mu * rho

where N is population size, mu is the mutation rate per individual per 
generation, and rho is the fixation probability.

This single equation contains both major theories of molecular evolution:

**Neutral theory (Kimura, 1968):** When mutations are selectively neutral 
(r = 1), the fixation probability is rho = 1/N. Plugging in:

    R = N * mu * (1/N) = mu

The N's cancel. The substitution rate equals the mutation rate, regardless 
of population size. This is why a mouse and an elephant accumulate neutral 
DNA changes at roughly the same rate — it's the foundation of the 
molecular clock used in phylogenetics.

**Adaptive (Darwinian) evolution:** When mutations are beneficial (r > 1), 
fixation probability is approximately 2(r - 1) for large N, so:

    R ≈ 2 * N * mu * (r - 1)

Now the rate scales with N. Larger populations adapt faster because they 
sample more beneficial mutations. This is why bacterial populations evolve 
antibiotic resistance so quickly — a single infection can contain 10^10 
cells, and the rate of beneficial fixations grows linearly with that.

The two regimes coexist in real genomes: most molecular changes are 
roughly neutral and fix at rate mu, while a smaller fraction are 
adaptive and fix at a rate that depends on population size.




## The plots

This repo includes three figures generated by `moran_simulation.py`. 
Each one shows a different idea from the theory above.

### 1. `moran_trajectories.png` — random luck in evolution



This plot shows 30 separate runs of the Moran process. Each run starts 
with one mutant in a population of 100, and the mutant has a 10% fitness 
advantage (r = 1.1).

- **Green lines** = the mutant took over (good outcome)
- **Red lines** = the mutant went extinct (bad outcome)

Even though the mutant is fitter, most runs end in extinction. Why? 
When there's only one mutant, random death can easily wipe it out 
before it has a chance to grow. Theory predicts only about 9% of 
beneficial mutants will fix, and the simulation matches that.

**Key idea:** Being fitter doesn't guarantee survival. You also have 
to get lucky early on.


### 2. `moran_fixation_vs_r.png` — does the formula work?



This plot tests whether the fixation probability formula is correct.

- **Black curve** = what the formula predicts
- **Red dots** = what actually happened in 2000 simulations

The dots land right on the curve, so the formula is verified.

Reading the plot left to right:
- **Left side (r < 1):** Bad mutations almost never fix. The line 
  drops sharply.
- **Middle (r = 1):** Neutral mutations fix at probability 1/N. 
  This is the dotted reference line.
- **Right side (r > 1):** Good mutations fix more often, but even 
  doubling fitness only gives 50% odds.

**Key idea:** The math works. We can predict fixation chances 
without running simulations.


### 3. `moran_substitution_rate.png` — neutral vs Darwinian evolution



This plot shows how fast evolution happens as population size grows.

- **Blue (flat) line** = neutral mutations
- **Other lines** = beneficial mutations of different strengths

Two things to notice:

**The blue line is flat.** No matter how big the population, neutral 
mutations fix at the same rate (= mu, the mutation rate). This is 
Kimura's neutral theory and the basis of the molecular clock — it's 
why we can date when species split apart by counting DNA differences.

**The other lines climb upward.** Beneficial mutations fix faster in 
bigger populations. This is why bacteria evolve antibiotic resistance 
so quickly — billions of cells means lots of chances for a useful 
mutation to appear and spread.

**Key idea:** One equation (R = N · mu · rho) explains both kinds of 
evolution. Whether you get the neutral pattern or the Darwinian 
pattern depends on whether the mutation affects fitness.
